

\chapter{Introduction}
\label{cha:introduction}

The evolution of Deep Learning (DL) has produced a paradigm shift in artificial
intelligence in which predictive performance frequently comes at the cost of
transparency, a tension commonly described as the interpretability--performance
trade-off \cite{doshi2017rigorous}. Modern neural networks routinely match or
exceed human accuracy on perceptual tasks, yet their internal decision processes
remain opaque ``black boxes'' whose outputs cannot be inspected directly
\cite{adebayo2018sanity}. The field of Explainable AI (XAI) responds to this
opacity with a family of Feature Attribution Explanation (FAE) methods that
assign an importance score to each input feature, producing saliency or
attribution maps intended to reveal which parts of an input drove a given
prediction \cite{selvaraju2017grad, ribeiro2016why}. As these methods migrate
from research prototypes into high-stakes deployments, the question of whether a
particular explanation can actually be trusted has become as important as the
question of whether a model is accurate. This thesis addresses that question
directly: rather than proposing a new attribution method, it treats the
\emph{evaluation} of attribution methods as the central problem and develops a
multi-criteria framework for assessing FAE methods rigorously and reproducibly.

\section{Research motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

The motivation for this work is driven by three interrelated factors: a legal
and ethical imperative for auditable AI, a methodological crisis in how
explanations are evaluated, and the particular difficulty of establishing
ground truth for explanations in the medical domain.

\subsection{The legal and ethical imperative}

Transparency in automated decision-making is no longer merely a desirable
technical property; it has become a regulatory requirement. The European Union's
Artificial Intelligence Act mandates that high-risk AI systems be designed and
developed so that their operation is sufficiently transparent to enable
deployers to interpret a system's output and use it appropriately
\cite{eu2024aiact}. The General Data Protection Regulation reinforces this
trajectory by establishing provisions, frequently summarised as a
``right to explanation'', that entitle individuals to meaningful information
about the logic of automated decisions that significantly affect them
\cite{eu2016gdpr}. Medical diagnostics is a paradigmatic high-risk setting: a
classifier that distinguishes malignant from benign skin lesions cannot be
adopted clinically unless its reasoning can be examined and audited, because a
physician cannot responsibly defer to a prediction whose basis cannot be
verified \cite{sangwan2024quantifying}. Crucially, the regulatory demand for
transparency is only meaningful if the explanations supplied to satisfy it are
themselves trustworthy. An attribution map that is plausible-looking but
unfaithful to the model offers a false sense of accountability and may be worse
than no explanation at all. This observation shifts the burden from
\emph{producing} explanations to \emph{evaluating} them, and motivates the need
for quantitative, defensible criteria by which an explanation's quality can be
judged.

\subsection{The crisis of evaluation}

Despite a rapid proliferation of XAI methods, the field suffers from what has
been termed a crisis of evaluation: there is little consensus on how to measure
whether an explanation is good, and the metrics that do exist often disagree
\cite{hedstrom2023quantus}. Foundational work has shown that several widely used
attribution methods fail elementary sanity checks, producing visually
convincing maps that remain essentially unchanged even when the parameters of
the model being explained are randomised, which means those maps cannot be
reflecting the learned function at all \cite{adebayo2018sanity}. Compounding
this, different methods frequently attribute importance to different features
for the same prediction, a phenomenon documented as systematic disagreement
across explanation methods \cite{krishna2024disagreement}; gradient-based
estimators, for example, tend to score well on faithfulness yet poorly on
robustness, while perturbation-based estimators exhibit the opposite profile
\cite{hryniewska2024normensemblexai}. A natural response to such
single-estimator unreliability is to combine several attribution methods into an
ensemble whose aggregated map is more stable than any of its constituents, as in
the NormEnsembleXAI approach \cite{hryniewska2024normensemblexai}.

Ensembling, however, only relocates the evaluation problem rather than solving
it, because one must still decide \emph{which} quality criteria to optimise and
\emph{how} to weigh them. A large and heterogeneous catalogue of evaluation
metrics now exists, organised by toolkits such as Quantus into categories
spanning Faithfulness, Robustness, Localization, Complexity, Randomization, and
Axiomatic correctness \cite{hedstrom2023quantus, paccotacya2024explanations}.
These categories are not independent, and some metrics within a category are
highly correlated, so naively reporting many scores can give an illusion of
thoroughness while measuring the same underlying property repeatedly
\cite{bommer2024finding}. Worse, the metrics themselves are not equally
reliable: a metric can be insensitive to perturbations it ought to detect, or
reactive to changes it ought to ignore, and meta-evaluation procedures have been
developed precisely to quantify this reliability \cite{hedstrom2024metaquantus,
tomsett2020sanity}. Evaluating FAE methods responsibly therefore requires a
framework that (i) prunes redundant metrics, (ii) discounts metrics that are
themselves unreliable, and (iii) aggregates the remaining, validated criteria
into a single comparable effectiveness signal. Because this aggregation balances
several conflicting quality dimensions simultaneously, the evaluation is
inherently \emph{multi-objective} in the space of metrics, even though the
predictive models being explained are ordinary single-output classifiers.

\subsection{Ground truth in the medical domain}

A further difficulty specific to high-stakes vision tasks is that most metrics
of the Localization family presuppose a spatial reference against which an
attribution map can be compared, yet such ground truth is rarely available.
General-purpose image benchmarks supply only image-level class labels, leaving
no principled way to ask whether an explanation highlights the
\emph{semantically correct} region. The medical-imaging setting alleviates this
problem in a way that aligns naturally with the regulatory motivation above:
expert-annotated lesion masks delineate exactly the region a clinically valid
explanation ought to emphasise, and these masks therefore serve as an objective
spatial reference for Localization metrics without any additional labelling
effort \cite{sangwan2024quantifying}. The ISIC 2017 dermoscopic dataset, used
throughout this thesis, provides such expert masks paired with each image, which
makes it possible to ground the Localization category empirically and gives the
evaluation framework a concrete, auditable, and ethically motivated testbed.

\section{Research contributions}
\label{sec:celePracy}

The unit of novelty in this thesis is the evaluation pipeline---the procedure by
which feature attribution methods are scored, validated, and ranked---rather
than any new explanation method or predictive architecture. Against the
background outlined above, the contributions of this work are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Non-redundancy analysis of FAE evaluation metrics.} An
    empirical correlation study across the six Quantus metric categories that
    identifies highly correlated metrics within each category and derives a
    compact, non-redundant representative subset, so that subsequent evaluation
    measures distinct quality dimensions rather than re-measuring the same
    property \cite{hedstrom2023quantus, bommer2024finding}.

    \item \textbf{A multi-criteria evaluation framework with a
    MetaQuantus-discounted aggregation.} A methodological framework that
    integrates MetaQuantus-style meta-validation---quantifying each metric's
    Noise Resilience and Adversarial Reactivity---with an Autoweighted
    aggregation scheme inspired by NormEnsembleXAI, and that proposes, as its
    central novel component, a MetaQuantus-discounted extension of Autoweighted
    aggregation in which the influence of a metric is attenuated in proportion
    to its measured unreliability \cite{hedstrom2024metaquantus,
    hryniewska2024normensemblexai}.

    \item \textbf{Empirical characterization of metric ensembling.} An analysis
    of when aggregation of metric scores produces FAE rankings that diverge from
    single-metric rankings, when it does not, and which metric families benefit
    most from aggregation, thereby clarifying the practical value added by
    multi-criteria evaluation over reporting individual metrics in isolation
    \cite{hedstrom2023quantus}.

    \item \textbf{Empirical validation on ISIC 2017 with statistical testing.}
    A practical assessment of the framework on the ISIC 2017 skin-lesion
    classification task, whose expert-annotated masks enable Localization
    metrics, comparing individual FAE methods---and individual versus
    NormEnsembleXAI-ensembled attributions---under the proposed aggregation, with
    statistical significance established through Friedman and Nemenyi tests
    across methods and Wilcoxon tests for paired comparisons
    \cite{sangwan2024quantifying, hryniewska2024normensemblexai}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Thesis contents}

The remainder of this thesis is structured as follows.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:sota}: Literature Review} provides an
    overview of the state of the art in Feature Attribution Explanation methods,
    detailing the taxonomy of gradient-based \cite{selvaraju2017grad} and
    perturbation-based \cite{ribeiro2016why} approaches. It establishes the
    crisis of evaluation \cite{hedstrom2023quantus} by discussing the failure of
    common saliency maps to pass model-sensitivity sanity checks
    \cite{adebayo2018sanity}, and it defines the mathematical foundations of the
    atomic metrics---Faithfulness, Robustness, Localization, Complexity, and
    related families---that serve as the building blocks for modular evaluation
    \cite{hedstrom2023quantus, alvarez2018robustness}.

    \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}: Methodology} introduces the
    proposed multi-criteria evaluation framework. It describes how heterogeneous
    attribution outputs are aligned through Second Moment Scaling normalization
    and combined, where ensembling is applied, via the NormEnsembleXAI
    aggregation functions \cite{hryniewska2024normensemblexai}; it specifies the
    metric set across the six Quantus categories and the redundancy analysis used
    to prune it; and it details the Autoweighted aggregation together with the
    novel MetaQuantus-discounted weighting that down-weights unreliable metrics
    \cite{hedstrom2024metaquantus, hryniewska2024normensemblexai}.

    \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments}: Experiments and Results}
    presents the empirical validation of the framework on the ISIC 2017
    dermoscopic dataset \cite{sangwan2024quantifying}, whose expert-annotated
    lesion masks enable Localization metrics. It reports the metric redundancy
    analysis, the MetaQuantus-based reliability of each metric, the comparison of
    uniform, Autoweighted, and MetaQuantus-discounted aggregation, the contrast
    between individual and NormEnsembleXAI-ensembled attributions
    \cite{hryniewska2024normensemblexai}, and the statistical significance of the
    resulting rankings via Friedman, Nemenyi, and Wilcoxon testing.

    \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}: Discussion} interprets the
    rankings, examines the threats to validity---notably the sample-size
    limitation of the redundancy and reliability estimates---and delimits the
    scope within which the conclusions generalise.

    \item \textbf{Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions}: Conclusions and Future Work}
    summarises the findings against each contribution, discusses the trade-offs
    between interpretability and performance \cite{doshi2017rigorous} and the
    computational cost of multi-criteria evaluation
    \cite{hryniewska2024normensemblexai}, and outlines future research into
    automated metric validation and explanation auditing within the scope of the
    EU AI Act.
\end{itemize}
