\chapter{Experiments and Results}
\label{ch:experiments}

This chapter reports the empirical evaluation of the framework developed in
Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}. The experiments are organised to mirror the four
research contributions of Section~\ref{sec:celePracy}:
Section~\ref{sec:exp-setup} fixes the experimental configuration;
Section~\ref{sec:exp-redundancy} presents the metric redundancy analysis
(Contribution~1); Section~\ref{sec:exp-meta} reports the meta-evaluation of
metric reliability (Contribution~2); Section~\ref{sec:exp-ranking} compares the
uniform, Autoweighted, and MetaQuantus-discounted aggregation schemes
(Contributions~2--3); Section~\ref{sec:exp-ensemble} contrasts individual and
NormEnsembleXAI-ensembled attributions (Contribution~3); and
Section~\ref{sec:exp-stats} establishes statistical significance via Friedman,
Nemenyi, and Wilcoxon testing (Contribution~4).

All quantitative tables in this chapter are generated directly from the
result artifacts under \texttt{results/} by
\texttt{experiments/render\_results\_tables.py} and \texttt{\textbackslash
input}-ed here, so the numbers below are the full-run values over the 600
ISIC~2017 test images. Two limitations are stated up front and revisited at the
point of use: (i)~in this run the MQ-discount weighting is numerically
identical to the Autoweighted scheme, because the per-metric meta-evaluation
reliability was only partially available (see Section~\ref{sec:exp-meta}), so
no reliability discount was applied; and (ii)~the attribution-ensembling
experiment of Section~\ref{sec:exp-ensemble} was run on a 64-image sample over
six gradient methods (Occlusion excluded for tractability) and scored on the
five non-Robustness $M^{*}$ metrics. Cells reading \textit{n/a} denote a value
that is genuinely undefined (e.g.\ a single-metric category, or a metric whose
Adversarial Reactivity could not be scored), not a missing computation.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp-setup}

\subsection{Data and Splits}

All experiments use the ISIC~2017 Challenge dataset (Task~1) described in
Section~\ref{sec:dataset}: 2{,}000 training, 150 validation, and 600 test
dermoscopic images across three diagnostic classes (melanoma, nevus,
seborrheic keratosis), each paired with an expert-annotated binary lesion
mask. The redundancy, meta-evaluation, aggregation, and statistical analyses
are all computed on the test split. The 12-image pilot reported below is a
fixed, reproducible subset of the test split used to validate the pipeline
plumbing prior to the full run.

\section{Metric Redundancy Analysis (Contribution~1)}
\label{sec:exp-redundancy}

This section addresses whether the twelve metrics carry independent signal or
duplicate one another. Following Decision~D4, for each model the metrics are
first variance-pre-screened (a metric is dropped if, within a model group, the
non-NaN fraction falls below 0.20 or the standard deviation falls below
$10^{-6}$), then the Spearman rank-correlation matrix is computed across the
test images, and pairs with $|\rho| > 0.85$ are greedily pruned, retaining in
each pair the metric less correlated with the remainder. The surviving set is
the reduced, non-redundant subset $M^{*}$ that feeds the aggregation stage.

\paragraph{Pre-screen.}
Two metrics are removed before correlation analysis on structural grounds:
\emph{Completeness} (constant at $0.0$ for the methods that satisfy the axiom,
hence zero-variance and uninformative for ranking) and \emph{Non-Sensitivity}
(disabled by default due to $O(d)$ forward-pass cost). The remaining ten
metrics enter the matrix. The exclusion of Completeness is justified by the
axiom-verification result (Table~\ref{tab:axiom}) rather than by correlation.

\paragraph{Correlation matrices.}
Tables~\ref{tab:redundancy-resnet} and~\ref{tab:redundancy-squeeze} hold the
within-category redundancy summaries for ResNet-18 and SqueezeNet, derived from
\texttt{results/redundancy\_matrix\_resnet18.csv} and
\texttt{results/redundancy\_matrix\_squeezenet.csv}. The full $10 \times 10$
Spearman matrices are reproduced in those CSVs; only the summary and the pruned
pairs are shown here for legibility.

\paragraph{Pruning outcome and $M^{*}$.}
On the full 600-image run the Complexity pair (complexity / sparseness,
$|\rho| \approx 0.99$ on both models) and the Robustness pair
(avg-/max-sensitivity, $|\rho| \approx 0.93$ on ResNet-18 and $0.98$ on
SqueezeNet) are strongly redundant, whereas the Faithfulness and Localization
pairs are nearly orthogonal ($|\rho| < 0.25$ in every case). The greedy
$|\rho| > 0.85$ pruning therefore drops one metric from each redundant pair
in both models (complexity in favour of sparseness; avg-sensitivity in favour
of max-sensitivity). The resulting reduced set $M^{*}$ is \emph{identical} for
the two models and comprises the same seven metrics:
\emph{faithfulness\_correlation}, \emph{max\_sensitivity},
\emph{pixel\_flipping}, \emph{pointing\_game}, \emph{random\_logit},
\emph{relevance\_mass\_accuracy}, and \emph{sparseness}
(Tables~\ref{tab:redundancy-resnet}--\ref{tab:redundancy-squeeze}). That the
two architectures yield the same $M^{*}$ is the principal evidence for
Contribution~1: the redundancy structure of these metrics is a property of the
metric battery rather than of the classifier. Table~\ref{tab:mstar} records
the set carried forward into aggregation.

\section{Meta-Evaluation: Metric Reliability (Contribution~2)}
\label{sec:exp-meta}

The redundancy analysis identifies which metrics overlap; meta-evaluation
identifies which metrics can be \emph{trusted}. Each metric in $M^{*}$ is
subjected to the two MetaQuantus stress tests of
Section~\ref{subsec:meta-evaluation}: Noise Resilience (NR), measuring score
stability under semantically meaningless input/weight perturbation, and
Adversarial Reactivity (AR), measuring whether the score drops monotonically as
the explanation is deliberately degraded. The combined reliability
$r_k = \tfrac{1}{2}(\mathrm{NR}_k + \mathrm{AR}_k)$ is the discount coefficient
used in Equation~\eqref{eq:mq-discount}.

Table~\ref{tab:meta} reports NR, AR, and combined reliability per metric,
averaged over the (model, FAE) cells in which each metric is defined, read from
\texttt{results/meta\_evaluation\_reliability.csv}. The accompanying runtime
column documents the cost of each metric and motivates the exclusion of the
most expensive metrics from default runs.

\input{tables/meta}

The reliability table is read as follows: a metric with high NR but low AR is
stable but insensitive (it does not react when the explanation degrades); a
metric with low NR is noisy. Among the $M^{*}$ metrics, \emph{sparseness}
($r_k = 0.975$), \emph{pixel\_flipping} ($0.883$), and \emph{pointing\_game}
($0.805$) are the most reliable, combining high noise resilience with strong
adversarial reactivity. \emph{Faithfulness\_correlation} ($0.540$) and
\emph{relevance\_mass\_accuracy} ($0.520$) are the least reliable: both are
noise-resilient only moderately (NR $\approx 0.51$--$0.55$) and react weakly to
deliberate degradation. The runtime column also explains the default
configuration: \emph{pixel\_flipping} ($732$\,s) and
\emph{faithfulness\_correlation} ($555$\,s) dominate the cost, motivating their
optional exclusion on tighter budgets.

\paragraph{Caveat: partial reliability and the MQ-discount.}
Two of the seven $M^{*}$ metrics, \emph{max\_sensitivity} and
\emph{random\_logit}, have no combined reliability (\textit{n/a} in
Table~\ref{tab:meta}): their Adversarial Reactivity test relies on an
\texttt{explain\_func} re-invocation that failed during the run, leaving only
the Noise Resilience leg. Because the discount coefficient $r_k$ could not be
formed for the full $M^{*}$, the MQ-discounted aggregation of
Section~\ref{sec:exp-ranking} was \emph{not} applied with a non-trivial
discount in this run; the \texttt{effectiveness\_mqdiscount} column therefore
equals the Autoweighted column exactly, and the two are reported as a single
scheme throughout. Closing this gap (re-running the failed AR leg for all
$M^{*}$ metrics) is left to future work.

\section{Aggregation and Ranking Comparison (Contributions~2--3)}
\label{sec:exp-ranking}

This section measures whether \emph{how} metrics are combined changes the
conclusion about which FAE method is best. Three weighting schemes are applied
to the same normalised scores over $M^{*}$:
(i)~\textbf{uniform} ($w_k = 1/|M^{*}|$);
(ii)~\textbf{Autoweighted} (weights from ensemble agreement $\mathrm{ES}_k$);
(iii)~\textbf{MQ-discounted} (Equation~\eqref{eq:mq-discount}, agreement
attenuated by reliability). A single-metric baseline (Faithfulness Correlation
alone, \texttt{effectiveness\_single\_fc}) is included to quantify how far a
naive one-metric ranking departs from the multi-criteria index. All four columns
are present in \texttt{results/ranking\_comparison.csv}. As noted in
Section~\ref{sec:exp-meta}, the MQ-discount column is numerically identical to
the Autoweighted column in this run (no reliability discount was applied), so
the two coincide in every table below.

\paragraph{The ranking diverges with the weighting scheme.}
The headline finding of Contributions~2--3 is that \emph{which FAE method ranks
first depends on how the metrics are combined.} On ResNet-18
(Table~\ref{tab:ranking-resnet}) Grad-CAM is the single best method under the
uniform scheme ($E = 0.370$, ahead of occlusion at $0.348$ and saliency at
$0.309$), but under the Autoweighted scheme it falls to fifth ($0.303$) while
saliency rises to first ($0.350$). The induced rankings are correspondingly
weakly correlated: the Spearman rank correlation between the uniform and
Autoweighted per-method orderings is only $\rho = 0.286$ for ResNet-18, with
ten discordant method-pairs (Table~\ref{tab:rank-stability}). SqueezeNet is far
more stable---Grad-CAM is first under both schemes ($E = 0.360$ uniform,
$0.250$ Autoweighted) and the uniform/Autoweighted orderings agree closely
($\rho = 0.964$). The single-metric baseline (Faithfulness Correlation alone)
diverges most sharply of all, reordering the methods almost entirely
(Single-FC vs.\ Autoweighted $\rho = 0.321$ on ResNet-18, $0.893$ on
SqueezeNet), which confirms that a one-metric ranking is not a safe proxy for
the multi-criteria index. This sensitivity to the weighting choice---present on
one architecture and largely absent on the other---is precisely the phenomenon
the framework is designed to surface; its interpretation is carried into
Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}.

\section{Individual vs.\ Ensembled Attributions (Contribution~3)}
\label{sec:exp-ensemble}

Beyond comparing individual methods, the framework is applied to attributions
produced by NormEnsembleXAI (Decision~D6: Mean aggregation with Second Moment
normalisation). The hypothesis under test is that an ensembled attribution
achieves a higher---or at least more balanced---effectiveness index than its
constituent individual methods, particularly on the Robustness axis where
single methods are known to be unstable.

\input{tables/ensemble}

\paragraph{The ensemble beats the average method but not the best one.}
Table~\ref{tab:ensemble} shows a consistent two-sided result on both models.
The ensembled attribution comfortably exceeds the \emph{mean} individual
method---$E = 0.700$ vs.\ $0.554$ on ResNet-18 and $0.812$ vs.\ $0.604$ on
SqueezeNet---so ensembling is a robust improvement over picking a method at
random. It does not, however, beat the \emph{best} individual method on either
model ($\Delta = -0.197$ on ResNet-18, $-0.076$ on SqueezeNet): an oracle that
already knew which single method was strongest would still win. The Wilcoxon
tests of Section~\ref{sec:exp-stats} (Table~\ref{tab:wilcoxon}) confirm that
both gaps are statistically significant. The practical reading is that
ensembling is a sound default when one cannot identify the best method in
advance, rather than a strict improvement over the per-model optimum.

\paragraph{Caveat: ensemble evaluation scope.}
This comparison was run on a 64-image sample (rather than the full 600) over the
six gradient-based methods only; Occlusion was excluded because perturbation
attributions are too costly to ensemble at scale. Consequently the ensemble
Robustness metrics were not computed, and the effectiveness index above is
formed over the five non-Robustness $M^{*}$ metrics. The result should be read
as indicative of the ensembling trade-off rather than as a full-scale,
all-metric verdict.

Per-axis effectiveness (one effectiveness value per metric category for the
ensembled vs.\ individual attributions) is reported in
Figure~\ref{fig:ensemble-radar} so that trade-offs---e.g.\ a gain in Robustness
purchased at the cost of Localization---are visible rather than hidden by the
scalar index.

\section{Statistical Significance (Contribution~4)}
\label{sec:exp-stats}

The final stage tests whether the observed ranking differences are
statistically significant rather than artefacts of sampling
(Decision~D5). Two complementary procedures are applied to the per-image
effectiveness distributions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Friedman test $+$ Nemenyi post-hoc} across all FAE methods.
    The Friedman test detects whether at least one method differs in mean rank
    across the test images; the Nemenyi post-hoc identifies which pairs differ,
    visualised as a critical-difference (CD) diagram.
    \item \textbf{Wilcoxon signed-rank test} for the paired comparison of
    individual vs.\ NormEnsembleXAI-ensembled attributions
    (Section~\ref{sec:exp-ensemble}), which is inherently paired per image.
\end{itemize}

\input{tables/friedman}

The Friedman omnibus test rejects the null hypothesis of equal method ranks
decisively on both models (Table~\ref{tab:friedman}): ResNet-18 yields
$\chi^2 = 437.4$ and SqueezeNet $\chi^2 = 1551.8$, both with $p < 10^{-3}$ over
the 600 image blocks under the Autoweighted scheme (identical to MQ-discount
here). At least one FAE method therefore differs significantly in mean rank, so
the Nemenyi post-hoc and CD diagram (Figure~\ref{fig:cd-diagram}) are
warranted; the per-method mean ranks feeding that diagram are in
\texttt{results/cd\_summary.csv}.

\input{tables/wilcoxon}

The paired Wilcoxon tests (Table~\ref{tab:wilcoxon}, $n = 64$ per model)
quantify both sides of the ensemble result of Section~\ref{sec:exp-ensemble}.
The ensemble significantly outperforms the \emph{mean} individual method on both
models (ResNet-18 $W = 384$, $p < 10^{-3}$; SqueezeNet $W = 111$,
$p < 10^{-3}$), and is significantly \emph{outperformed} by the \emph{best}
individual method (ResNet-18 $W = 347$, $p < 10^{-3}$; SqueezeNet $W = 682$,
$p = 0.017$). All four comparisons are significant at $\alpha = 0.05$, so the
two-sided conclusion---ensembling beats the average but not the oracle-best
method---is statistically robust rather than a sampling artefact.

The broader interpretation of these tests is carried into the discussion of
Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}.
