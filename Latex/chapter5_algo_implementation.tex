\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch:discussion}

This chapter interprets the experimental results of
Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments} in the light of the research questions, examines
the threats to the validity of the conclusions, and delimits the scope within
which the findings can be generalised. The discussion is organised so that the
contribution of the proposed evaluation framework can be appraised
\emph{independently} of any particular ranking it produces: the framework is a
methodological artefact whose value lies in how it structures the comparison,
and that value does not stand or fall with the specific ordering of FAE methods
that the full run reports.

\section{Interpretation of the Rankings}
\label{sec:disc-rankings}

This section interprets what the aggregated rankings of
Section~\ref{sec:exp-ranking} imply for practitioners selecting an FAE method.
The empirical yield is best read as a set of \emph{cautionary} findings: the
framework surfaces real instabilities in how FAE methods are ranked, and the
two literature-derived enhancements it tests deliver a model-dependent rather
than universal benefit. Each item below states the verdict the full 600-image
run returns.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Does multi-criteria aggregation change the verdict?}
    Yes---but conditionally. Comparing the single-metric baseline (Faithfulness
    Correlation alone) against the multi-criteria index
    (Table~\ref{tab:rank-stability}) shows that a one-metric ranking is not a
    safe proxy for the aggregate: the Single-FC and MQ-discounted orderings
    are essentially uncorrelated on ResNet-18 ($\rho = -0.14$) and correlate
    imperfectly on
    SqueezeNet ($\rho = 0.86$). A method that leads on Faithfulness alone is not
    guaranteed to survive once Robustness, Localization, and Randomization enter
    the aggregate. This vindicates the multi-criteria premise of the thesis: the
    scalar leaderboard one reads off a single faithfulness metric can differ
    materially from the one a balanced index returns.
    \item \textbf{Effect of the weighting scheme.} The headline---and the most
    important cautionary finding---is that \emph{how much the FAE ranking
    changes with the weighting scheme is itself model-dependent}. On ResNet-18
    the uniform and Autoweighted method-rankings agree only moderately
    (Spearman $\rho = 0.75$, five discordant method-pairs): Occlusion stays
    first under both schemes, but Grad-CAM drops from second under uniform
    weights to fourth under Autoweighted while Saliency and Integrated
    Gradients move up. On SqueezeNet the same two schemes agree almost
    perfectly ($\rho = 0.96$), with Grad-CAM first under both. The weighting
    choice can therefore reorder much of the field on one architecture while
    leaving the other nearly untouched. The
    most plausible mechanism is the inverse-variance character of the
    Autoweighted scheme: its weights are driven by each model's per-metric
    score-variance structure, so two models with different variance profiles
    redistribute weight across $M^{*}$ differently, and only where that
    redistribution is large does the ranking move. This is evidence that
    aggregation \emph{can} reorder rankings materially, not that it reliably
    does---a warning
    against trusting any single-number FAE leaderboard, but not a licence to
    assume the weighting always matters.
    \item \textbf{Does the MetaQuantus reliability discount change anything?}
    Yes---moderately, and model-dependently, once the meta-validation is
    complete. With both reliability legs of all seven $M^{*}$ metrics
    measured, the MQ-discounted ordering departs substantially from
    Autoweighted on
    ResNet-18 ($\rho = 0.46$: LRP rises from fifth into a statistical tie
    with Occlusion at the top---the top-2 \emph{set} is stable in every
    jackknife replicate but not under every $\pm0.2$ reliability shift,
    and their mutual order is unresolved throughout---and
    Grad-CAM
    falls into the bottom cluster) while SqueezeNet moves only mildly
    ($\rho = 0.96$); the mean effectiveness shift is $|\Delta|\approx0.14$
    on ResNet-18 and $0.06$ on SqueezeNet.
    Mechanically, the discount redistributes weight toward the
    high-reliability metrics (sparseness $r_k = 0.97$, max-sensitivity
    $0.96$, pixel-flipping $0.88$) and away from
    random-logit ($0.63$---near-perfect noise resilience but reactivity at
    the noise floor, the textbook stable-but-insensitive case),
    faithfulness-correlation ($0.58$), and relevance-mass
    accuracy ($0.55$). The discount is therefore a consequential modelling
    choice on one architecture and nearly inert on the other---the same
    conditional pattern as the uniform-vs-Autoweighted comparison
    (Section~\ref{sec:disc-redundancy}).
    \item \textbf{Per-category trade-offs.} The ensembling comparison
    (Section~\ref{sec:exp-ensemble}) shows that NormEnsembleXAI does not buy
    any improvement on this task: on both models the ensemble trades gains on
    some metric
    categories for losses on others rather than dominating, and once ensemble
    and individual scores are placed on a common scale its scalar
    effectiveness beats none of the reported baselines---significantly worse
    than the mean individual method on ResNet-18, indistinguishable from it
    on SqueezeNet, and decisively below the best fixed method on both. A
    nominal SqueezeNet advantage under the earlier per-source normalization
    proved to be an artefact of that normalization choice, which is exactly
    why the per-category profiles and the normalization protocol are reported
    alongside the scalar.
    \item \textbf{Cross-model consistency.} The two architectures agree on the
    one result that matters most for Contribution~1---the reduced metric set
    $M^{*}$ is \emph{identical} across ResNet-18 and SqueezeNet---but disagree on
    almost everything downstream: the ranking divergence is large on ResNet-18
    and negligible on SqueezeNet, and the ensemble is significantly worse than
    the average method on ResNet-18 yet indistinguishable from it
    on SqueezeNet. Persistent agreement on $M^{*}$ strengthens the claim that the
    redundancy structure is a metric-battery property; persistent disagreement
    elsewhere is itself the finding, and a caution against treating any single
    model's ranking as the framework's verdict.
\end{itemize}

\section{Implications of the Redundancy and Reliability Findings}
\label{sec:disc-redundancy}

The redundancy and reliability analyses are complementary filters acting on the
same metric set, and their interaction is the point of methodological interest.
On the full 600-image run the two filters give different yields: the
redundancy filter is the strongest and cleanest result of the thesis, while
the reliability filter---once turned into the MQ-discount---produces a
moderate, architecture-dependent reweighting of the ranking.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Redundant categories.} The full-run correlation matrix shows
    two metric pairs collapsing to a single effective dimension on \emph{both}
    architectures: Complexity (complexity~$\leftrightarrow$~sparseness,
    $|\rho| = 0.999$ on ResNet-18 and $0.993$ on SqueezeNet) and Robustness
    (avg-sensitivity~$\leftrightarrow$~max-sensitivity, $|\rho| = 0.93$ and
    $0.98$ respectively), whereas the Faithfulness and Localization pairs remain
    nearly orthogonal ($|\rho| < 0.25$). The practical implication is concrete:
    reporting both members of a near-collinear pair overstates the evidence,
    because the two metrics constitute one vote, not two---an evaluator who
    quotes ``twelve metrics'' is in effect quoting roughly seven independent
    ones. That the greedy pruning yields an \emph{identical} seven-metric
    $M^{*}$ on two different architectures is the strongest single finding here:
    the redundancy structure is shared across the two studied classifiers
    rather than idiosyncratic to either---though with two CNNs on one
    dataset this is evidence of consistency, not proof of
    classifier-independence. A practitioner can therefore halve the costly
    Robustness
    leg (avg-sensitivity pruned---the sensitivity metrics are among the most
    expensive in the battery, as each score re-invokes the explanation
    function ten times per image) and
    drop Complexity's duplicate, with no loss of discriminative signal; the two
    expensive Faithfulness metrics are retained, as both carry independent
    signal.
    \item \textbf{Greedy cross-category pruning.} The current pruning is greedy
    and can in principle drop a metric because it correlates with a metric in a
    \emph{different} category. On this data the consequence is benign---both
    pruned pairs are \emph{within} a single category (Complexity; Robustness),
    so the greedy policy implemented as Decision~D4 and a strictly
    within-category alternative coincide
    and yield the same $M^{*}$. The robustness of the result to that policy
    choice is reassuring, but it is a property of this dataset's correlation
    structure and not a guarantee; a strictly within-category policy remains the
    more defensible default and is recommended for transfer to other domains.
    \item \textbf{Reliability vs.\ agreement, and what the MQ-discount does.}
    The MetaQuantus reliability ordering and the dispersion-based Autoweighted
    ordering are conceptually distinct, and the thesis proposed the
    MQ-discount precisely to let the former attenuate the latter. Empirically,
    the discount is consequential exactly where the two orderings disagree
    most: on ResNet-18 it shifts the per-method effectiveness by
    $|\Delta|\approx0.14$ on average and reorders much of the field
    ($\rho(\text{Autoweighted},\text{MQ-discount}) = 0.46$; LRP fifth
    $\to$ statistical tie for first, Grad-CAM $\to$ bottom cluster), while
    on SqueezeNet it is mild
    ($\rho = 0.96$).
    Three caveats keep this claim honest. First, the AR leg for the two
    explanation-recomputing metrics required a protocol extension introduced
    by this thesis (degrading the explanation function itself,
    Section~\ref{sec:exp-meta}), so their reliability scores are measured
    under an extended, not the original, MetaQuantus protocol. Second,
    \emph{which} method leads the reordered field is not resolved: raising
    random-logit's reliability by $0.2$---well within the uncertainty of a
    reactivity measurement that sits at its noise floor---drops LRP from the
    leading pair to third (Section~\ref{sec:exp-meta}). The robust claim is
    that the discount reorders the field, not that it crowns LRP. Third, no
    ground truth says the MQ-discounted ranking is \emph{better}---the
    framework establishes that reliability weighting changes the verdict, not
    that the changed verdict is correct. The contribution is the measured
    answer to a question the literature had left assumed: a
    reliability-discounted aggregation \emph{can} change the FAE ranking, and
    whether it does is architecture-dependent.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Value of the Aggregation Framework Independent of the Rankings}
\label{sec:disc-framework-value}

A central claim of this thesis is that the proposed evaluation pipeline is a
contribution in its own right, separable from whichever FAE method it happens
to rank highest on ISIC~2017. Three arguments support reading the framework's
value independently of the specific rankings it produces.

First, the pipeline makes the configuration of an evaluation \emph{explicit and
reproducible}. The four-stage decomposition---redundancy-aware metric
selection, meta-validation, direction-rectified normalisation, and weighted
aggregation---separates the choices that an evaluator must make (which metrics,
which normalisation, which aggregator, which weighting) from the method under
test. Two researchers who disagree about the conclusion can now locate the
disagreement in a named configuration decision rather than in an opaque,
ad-hoc averaging of scores. This is precisely the property that
\citet{seth2025bridging} argue is required for an evaluation to function as
an auditable, compliance-grade instrument, and it holds whatever the rankings
turn out to be.

Second, the framework operationalises a defensible answer to the metric
disagreement documented in the literature. \citet{krishna2024disagreement} and
\citet{tomsett2020sanity} show that XAI metrics frequently disagree and that
the apparent rigour of reporting ``many'' metrics is illusory when those
metrics are correlated. The redundancy stage answers this by collapsing
correlated metrics to representatives; the meta-validation stage answers the
companion concern---that some metrics are simply unreliable
\citep{hedstrom2024metaquantus}---by discounting metrics that fail Noise
Resilience or Adversarial Reactivity. The MQ-discounted Autoweighted operator
fuses both filters into a single, data-adaptive weight derivation; whether
that fusion measurably changes the ranking is an empirical question, and on
this data the answer is architecture-dependent---material on ResNet-18,
marginal on SqueezeNet (Section~\ref{sec:disc-redundancy}). The value of the
framework here is that it let the question be \emph{asked and answered}
rather than assumed, and that the answer comes with its conditionality
attached. The
methodological claim---that aggregation should be preceded by redundancy pruning
and that reliability weighting is worth testing---is itself testable and
transferable whatever the leaderboard outcome on a particular dataset.

Third, the framework yields a \emph{stable apparatus for asking conditional
questions} rather than a single verdict. By reporting three aggregation
operators (weighted mean, minimum, geometric mean) and three weighting schemes,
the pipeline exposes how sensitive any conclusion is to the evaluator's
priorities. The minimum operator encodes a worst-dimension, safety-critical
stance; the geometric mean penalises catastrophic failure on any axis; the
weighted mean expresses a compensatory trade-off. A practitioner in a
high-stakes medical setting and a practitioner prototyping a research model can
read different recommendations off the same computation. The framework's
contribution is to make that conditionality visible and principled, which is
independent of the numerical outcome of any one configuration.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:disc-threats}

\subsection{Internal Validity}

Several design decisions could bias the conclusions drawn from within the study
itself, independently of how far they generalise.

The most consequential internal threat concerns \textbf{metric
failures and missing-value handling}. The Model Parameter Randomisation Test
(MPRT) proved incompatible with this dataset under Quantus~0.6.0: during the
pilot it yielded large numbers of \texttt{NaN} values---most acutely for
SqueezeNet and for Grad-CAM on the randomised ResNet---and on ISIC~2017 its
implementation ultimately failed on every (model, image) sample, so it was
disabled outright for the full run (Section~\ref{sec:metrics}). The decision
has since been vindicated by the metric's own authors, who document
methodological flaws in the original MPRT and propose repaired variants
\citep{hedstrom2024sanity}; a future run should adopt Smooth- or
Efficient-MPRT rather than the original test. When a metric
is computable on only a biased
subsample of images, any correlation or reliability estimate derived from it is
suspect: an apparent architectural difference in, say, the
MPRT--Sparseness relationship between the two models may be a genuine effect or
merely an artefact of which images happened to produce a finite score. For this
reason the Randomization category is represented in the aggregated index by
RandomLogit alone. Similarly, Non-Sensitivity is
excluded from the routine evaluation because its $O(d)$ forward-pass cost forces
aggressive input downsampling, which changes what the metric measures and makes
its scores non-comparable with the per-pixel metrics; it is reported, if at
all, only as a standalone diagnostic. Completeness is intrinsically defined only
for the axiomatic methods (Integrated Gradients, DeepLift, LRP) and returns
\texttt{NaN} elsewhere by construction; this is handled by per-metric masking
rather than imputation, so that no method is credited or penalised on an
inapplicable axis.

A second internal threat is the \textbf{quality of the predictors being
explained}. The two classifiers are only modestly accurate: ResNet-18 reaches
$75.3\%$ best validation accuracy and SqueezeNet $74.0\%$, while ISIC~2017 is
dominated by the nevus class; the validation split's majority-class
baseline is $52\%$ ($78/150$ nevus images), so both predictors clear their
own split's baseline by roughly twenty points---respectable but far from
strong. On the 600-image test split, where the majority baseline rises to
$65.5\%$ ($393/600$), the two diverge sharply on the plain-accuracy view
of the same predictions (\texttt{results/test\_set\_accuracy.csv}; the
balanced-accuracy summary is the one tabulated in
Table~\ref{tab:model-summary}): ResNet-18 holds at $74.2\%$, whereas
SqueezeNet drops to $55.0\%$ and therefore scores \emph{below} the
majority-class baseline, clearing it only on balanced accuracy
($63.7\%$, against $33.3\%$ for a constant-class predictor). For
SqueezeNet the attributions evaluated in Chapter~\ref{ch:experiments} are thus
explanations of a classifier that a trivial always-nevus rule would
out-score on plain accuracy. ResNet-18 moreover
shows clear overfitting ($98\%$ train accuracy against $75.3\%$ validation;
SqueezeNet is better balanced at $78.5\%$ train / $74.0\%$ validation). The
attributions therefore explain imperfect, partly over-fitted classifiers. This
is a genuine validity consideration---an explanation of a flawed model inherits
that model's flaws---but it is not fatal to the thesis, because the predictive
models are explicitly \emph{not} the contribution: the unit of novelty is the
evaluation pipeline, which is exercised identically whatever the classifier's
accuracy. The finding to carry forward is simply that the rankings reported here
characterise these two specific, imperfect models, not an idealised classifier.

A third internal threat is the \textbf{target-class convention}. Attributions
are computed for the model's predicted class rather than the ground-truth label.
This is the correct choice for a faithfulness study---the explanation should
describe the decision the model actually made---but it interacts with
misclassified images, where the explained logit corresponds to a wrong class and
the Localization metrics are scored against a mask that need not align with what
the model attended to. The measured error rates put a figure on this: $155$ of the
$600$ test images are misclassified by ResNet-18 ($25.8\%$) and $270$ by
SqueezeNet ($45.0\%$), so for nearly half the SqueezeNet evaluation the
explained logit belongs to a class the lesion mask does not depict. This
bounds the interpretability of the Localization results accordingly, and
does so far more tightly for SqueezeNet than for ResNet-18.

A fourth internal threat is the \textbf{extended AR protocol}, and it bears
directly on the foundation of the MQ-discount. The Adversarial Reactivity of
max-sensitivity and random-logit cannot be measured by degrading the
attribution array (both metrics re-compute attributions internally and ignore
it), so this thesis extends the MetaQuantus protocol: for those metrics the
degradation is injected into the explanation function itself
(Section~\ref{sec:exp-meta}). With the extension, all seven $M^{*}$ metrics
carry a measured combined reliability ($126$ of $168$ reliability rows are
valid; the invalid remainder belongs entirely to the three structurally
excluded metrics). Four caveats accumulate here. First, the extension is
this thesis's
own construction rather than part of the published MetaQuantus suite: the
degraded-explainer AR measures reaction to a \emph{stochastically corrupted
explanation process}, which is analogous to, but not identical with, the
original array-degradation test---and for the sensitivity metrics, whose very
definition compares repeated explainer invocations, the independently drawn
degradation masks mechanically induce part of the measured reactivity, so
max-sensitivity's top reliability rank should be read with particular
caution. Second, the reliability estimates rest on a single 64-image sample
with a
coarse-grained AR estimator, as disclosed in
Section~\ref{sec:exp-meta}; the leave-one-cell-out jackknife reported there
shows the top-2 set of the ResNet-18 reordering is not an artefact of any
single cell ($\{$LRP, Occlusion$\}$ in all seven replicates), though
neither their mutual order nor the
middle of the MQ-discounted field is resolvable at this
granularity. Third, the direction-agnostic $|\rho_S|$ awarded credit to one
of the
28 audited cells that reacted in the unexpected direction, and
random-logit's measured reactivity ($\mathrm{AR} = 0.27$) is
indistinguishable from the
noise floor
of a ten-level $|\rho_S|$ estimate---it may be a fully unreactive metric,
in which case even its discounted $0.23$ weight overstates it. Fourth, the
NR leg of the two extension metrics had to be re-measured after the silent
failure disclosed in Section~\ref{sec:exp-meta}, and the meta-evaluation's
targets were ResNet-18's predictions for both models (the two models
disagree on 25 of the 64 sampled images); the reliability estimand is
plausibly target-robust, but the SqueezeNet meta-evaluation has not been
repeated with its own targets. The reliability
scores---and therefore the ResNet-18 reordering the MQ-discount
produces---inherit these caveats, and validating the extension (shared
degradation masks per level, a direction-rectified AR, own-target
re-measurement, larger samples)
is listed as future work.

A fifth internal threat is \textbf{post-selection inference}. The same 600
test images supply the correlation matrix that selects $M^{*}$, the
normalisation statistics, the Autoweighted weights, and the per-image
effectiveness values on which the Friedman and Wilcoxon tests are run. The
significance tests therefore certify differences in a statistic whose
configuration was itself tuned on the tested data, and their nominal
$p$-values should be read in that light. Two mitigations apply: the
selection and weighting steps do not optimise for any particular method
ordering (they see only metric-level statistics, never the method
labels being ranked), and the observed $p$-values ($<10^{-10}$) leave a wide
margin. A held-out-split protocol---selecting $M^{*}$ and the weights on one
half of the test set and testing on the other---is the clean remedy and is
listed as future work.

\subsection{Construct Validity}

Construct validity asks whether the chosen metrics actually capture the quality
dimensions they are named for. The framework's premise is that the six Quantus
categories---Faithfulness, Robustness, Localization, Complexity, Randomization,
and Axiomatic correctness---together span the relevant notion of explanation
``effectiveness.'' Two residual risks remain even after redundancy pruning and
meta-validation.

The first is that meta-validation itself relies on proxies. Noise Resilience
and Adversarial Reactivity \citep{hedstrom2024metaquantus} are plausible
necessary conditions for a trustworthy metric, but they are not sufficient: a
metric can be stable under noise and reactive to explanation degradation while
still failing to track any human-meaningful sense of explanation quality.
Independent meta-evaluations sharpen the point: using transparent models with
ground-truth explanations, \citet{miro2024stability} find that Max- and
Avg-Sensitivity fail to flag randomly generated explanations---a more
critical verdict on the same Robustness metrics than the NR/AR tests reach
here, and a reminder that a metric's measured reliability depends on which
meta-evaluation lens is applied.
Because the entire study is functionally grounded---no clinician judgements
enter the loop---there is an irreducible gap between ``passes the
meta-evaluation stress tests'' and ``measures what a domain expert would call a
good explanation.'' The framework narrows this gap but cannot close it without
the human-grounded validation that is explicitly out of scope.

A related construct risk is that \textbf{metric implementations can emit
out-of-range or heavy-tailed raw scores}. Relevance Mass Accuracy is
nominally a mass fraction in $[0, 1]$, yet with signed attributions
(\texttt{abs=False}) its denominator---the total signed relevance---can
approach zero or change sign: $26\%$ of the valid full-run RMA values
exceed $1$ (maximum ${\approx}2 \times 10^{3}$, concentrated in Integrated
Gradients, DeepLift, Guided Backpropagation, and LRP) and a further
$18\%$ are \emph{negative}, so roughly $44\%$ of a nominal mass fraction
falls outside its own range---the negative values being the more troubling,
since a negative relevance mass has no interpretation as a fraction at
all. Max-Sensitivity similarly
explodes where the re-attributed explanations are unstable (for LRP on
ResNet-18 the median is ${\approx}52$ and the maximum ${\approx}1.9 \times
10^{5}$). Second Moment Scaling dampens but does not remove such heavy
tails---an outlier-inflated RMS deflates every other score in its
group---and this mechanism is precisely what produced the per-source
ensemble artefact of Section~\ref{sec:exp-ensemble}. Robust or winsorized
scaling, and an absolute-value RMA variant, are natural hardening steps for
future work.

The second construct risk concerns \textbf{normalisation}. Second Moment
Scaling was adopted from NormEnsembleXAI \citep{hryniewska2024normensemblexai}
because it aligns heterogeneous attribution scales without the outlier
sensitivity of min--max scaling, but the choice is a modelling assumption about
what makes two metric scores ``comparable.'' The ranking could in principle
shift under $z$-score or min--max normalisation; any conclusion that is not
stable across normalisers must be stated as normaliser-conditional. This is
not a hypothetical caveat: the individual-vs-ensemble verdict on SqueezeNet
changes from a nominally significant ensemble win to statistical
indistinguishability when the per-source scaling is replaced by a pooled
scale (Section~\ref{sec:exp-ensemble})---the clearest demonstration in this
thesis that a normalisation choice can manufacture or dissolve a finding.
Conversely, the per-(model, metric) grouping used here has independent
support: \citet{edin2025naopc} show that raw faithfulness scores are not
comparable across models at all unless normalised per model, which is exactly
the grouping this thesis applies before any cross-model statement is made.
This caveat
is sharpened by the nature of the effectiveness index itself.

The third construct risk is that \textbf{the effectiveness index is a relative,
normaliser-conditional, unbounded quantity, not an absolute quality score}.
Under the Autoweighted scheme it ranges roughly $-0.49$ to $0.61$ across
per-image evaluations,
and under the single-metric baseline it spans $-2.65$ to $4.02$; the sign and
magnitude depend on the chosen normaliser and on the set of methods being
compared. The index is therefore a \emph{ranking device}---it orders methods
within a fixed configuration---and must not be read as an absolute measure of
explanation quality, nor compared across configurations as though it lived on a
common scale. Every numerical effectiveness value reported in this thesis should
be interpreted in that relative sense.

\subsection{External Validity}

External validity concerns the reach of the conclusions beyond the precise
conditions of the experiment, and it is where the study is most deliberately
bounded.

All evidence comes from a \textbf{single dataset and domain}: ISIC~2017
dermoscopy. The medical domain was chosen deliberately---it supplies the
expert-annotated lesion masks that make the Localization category measurable at
all, and it grounds the regulatory motivation of the thesis---but a single
domain limits how far the redundancy and reliability \emph{structure} can be
assumed to transfer. The correlation between two faithfulness metrics, or the
reliability of MPRT, may differ on natural images or on a different imaging
modality; the framework is portable, but the particular $M^{*}$ and weight
vector it produces are not claimed to be.

The study uses \textbf{only two architectures}, ResNet-18 and SqueezeNet~1.1,
both convolutional. Transformer-based classifiers route gradients differently
and may interact differently with gradient-based attributions and with the
layer-wise randomisation that MPRT performs; nothing in this study speaks to
that regime. The two-model design does allow a minimal cross-architecture
consistency check, and it earns its keep: the identical $M^{*}$ across the two
models is the thesis's strongest transfer claim. But the same two-model design
sharply limits the reach of the \emph{divergence} findings. The headline result
that aggregation can reorder the FAE ranking rests substantially on a single
model---ResNet-18, where the uniform/Autoweighted correlation is $\rho = 0.75$,
against $\rho = 0.96$ on SqueezeNet---and the ensemble's standing likewise
differs between the two (significantly below the mean on ResNet-18,
indistinguishable from it on SqueezeNet).
A finding that is large on one of two architectures and negligible on the other
cannot be generalised; it is a demonstration that the phenomenon \emph{can}
occur, not a measurement of how often it does. A wider panel of architectures is
needed before the divergence is treated as anything more than possible.

Finally, the redundancy and reliability structure is estimated from a
\textbf{single finite sample from one challenge distribution}. Even at the full
$600$ images, confidence intervals on the correlation and reliability estimates
and a repeated-subsample stability analysis would be needed before the metric
structure reported here is treated as a property of the domain rather than of
this particular draw of images---and the reliability estimates for the two
explanation-recomputing metrics, obtained under the extended AR protocol,
should be read with the internal-validity caveat above attached.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:disc-limitations}

Beyond the validity threats above, several scope limitations bound the
contribution and are stated here so that the conclusions of
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions} are read in context.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Restricted method panel.} The computational cost of fitting
    per-instance surrogate models (LIME) and sampling Shapley coalitions
    (KernelSHAP)---a cost the Robustness metrics multiply, since they re-invoke
    the explanation function for every perturbation
    sample---constrained the evaluated panel to the seven methods
    retained in Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} (Integrated Gradients, Saliency,
    Grad-CAM, DeepLift, Guided Backpropagation, LRP, and Occlusion). The two
    perturbation-based surrogate methods are therefore deferred rather than
    dismissed, and their integration is identified as future work.
    \item \textbf{Map-level explanations only.} The framework evaluates
    attribution \emph{maps}. It does not address concept-level, prototype-based,
    or counterfactual explanations, whose quality criteria differ and would
    require a separate metric taxonomy.
    \item \textbf{Functionally grounded evaluation only.} Human-grounded
    validation---clinician studies of whether the highlighted regions are
    clinically sensible---is explicitly out of scope. The effectiveness index is
    a functional measure, and its alignment with expert judgement is an open
    question rather than an established result.
    \item \textbf{Aggregation to a scalar.} Collapsing a multi-criteria profile
    to a single effectiveness index is itself a modelling choice that can hide
    trade-offs between categories. The thesis mitigates this by reporting the
    underlying per-category profiles alongside the scalar index, but the scalar
    remains a summary, not a substitute for the profile.
\end{itemize}
